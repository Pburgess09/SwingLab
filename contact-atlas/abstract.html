PROJECT ABSTRACT / EXPLORATORY BASEBALL ANALYSIS

How does pitch height
shape the swing?

Separating the plane of the barrel’s approach from its direction at impact across successful four-seam hits.

THE WORKING HYPOTHESIS

A different approach.
A smaller change at impact.

Hitters adapt to pitch height by changing the orientation of the swing plane: flatter for higher pitches and steeper for lower pitches. The bat can still travel upward at contact in both cases, with a smaller change in attack angle than in swing-plane tilt.

This predicts that approach-plane tilt and attack angle need not change by the same magnitude—or in the same direction. A steeper plane on a low pitch does not automatically mean a greater upward attack angle at impact.

01 / BACKGROUND & OBJECTIVE

Define the movement before interpreting the cue.

Hitting discussions often use “swing up,” “swing down,” and “stay flat” to describe different parts of the movement. This project separates two measured quantities: swing-path tilt, the orientation of the plane traced by the barrel during the 40 milliseconds before contact, and attack angle, the vertical direction of the bat’s sweet spot at impact.

The objective was to determine whether higher pitch locations are associated with flatter swing planes within the same hitters, and to quantify how attack angle changes alongside that adjustment. Attack angle was not assumed to be identical across locations.

02 / TESTABLE PREDICTIONS

What would support the hypothesis?

  1. Plane adjustment: high pitches show lower swing-path tilt than low pitches.
  2. Impact distinction: the high-to-low change in attack angle is smaller than the change in tilt, with upward attack angles remaining common at both heights.
  3. Within-hitter consistency: the flatter-plane association remains when comparisons account for hitter, season, batting side and horizontal pitch location.

These predictions concern observed movement. They do not predict which verbal cue a hitter used or whether a cue improved performance.

03 / METHODS

Compare pitch heights within hitters.

Fresh MLB Statcast exports covered the 2024 and 2025 regular seasons. Eligibility was limited to four-seam fastballs resulting in singles, doubles, triples or home runs, with pitch centers inside the defined strike zone. Pitch height was normalized to each recorded strike zone and divided into low, middle and high thirds.

Of 24,224 unique downloaded hits, 18,986 were in the defined zone. Both angles were available for 17,660 hits from 705 hitters; 1,326 in-zone hits were excluded for missing angle measurements. The primary adjusted comparison used 17,384 hits from 625 hitters with within-group pitch-height variation and available controls.

A fixed-effects model assigned separate baselines to each hitter-season-batting-side combination and adjusted for horizontal location, its squared term, pitch velocity and pitcher handedness. Confidence intervals accounted for repeated observations by hitter. Secondary checks added contact depth and compared high and low pitches within the same hitter, season, batting side and horizontal third.

04 / RESULTS

The flatter-plane pattern appeared in both right- and left-handed hitters and persisted in the secondary comparisons. Pooled median attack angles remained upward at both heights: approximately +6.7° on low pitches and +8.3° on high pitches. Those pooled medians describe the sample; they are not the adjusted within-hitter estimates.

Attack angle changed less than swing-plane tilt, but was not identical. The analysis did not test statistical equivalence or establish an optimal attack angle.

05 / INTERPRETATION

The approach and impact tell different parts of the story.

The findings support a location-dependent adjustment in the barrel’s approach plane alongside a smaller change in its direction at impact. A flatter plane on high pitches can coexist with a slightly more upward attack angle. The two measurements should be interpreted together rather than treated as interchangeable descriptions of the swing.

Contact depth and where impact occurs along the swing arc are plausible contributors to this relationship. This analysis does not reconstruct the full barrel trajectory or isolate the mechanical cause of the observed pattern.

06 / LIMITATIONS & NEXT QUESTION

Movement evidence is not evidence of cue effectiveness.

This is an observational analysis of successful hits. It excludes misses, fouls and batted-ball outs, and missing tracking may introduce selection bias. It cannot establish causation, prescribe one swing for every hitter, or determine whether “swing down” or another cue improved performance.

A useful next study would pair hitter-reported cues with measured swings, including unsuccessful attempts, to examine whether an individual’s intended adjustment changes swing-plane tilt, attack angle and contact quality.

The original contact-depth atlas is a complementary study from an earlier import with 17,963 depth-eligible hits. Its different cohort should not be combined with the new complete-angle cohort as though they were the same dataset.
